---
title: Who may know what: the judgement we are about to delegate to machines
date: 2026-10-15
summary: Companies are pooling their knowledge into one layer that AI agents can read.
  Before they do, someone has to decide who may know what and answer for it,
  because an agent's default answer is yes.
card: Who may know what
---

Ask any client advisor at a bank how eager they are to document their work and their client book, and the answer will be clear: not particularly excited. The advisor’s knowledge of who calls whom, which negotiation is stuck, and which client tolerates which terms is bargaining power versus his employer, social capital with his clients, and finally economic capital which is portable across jobs. Sharing it strips him of all three. So, the book and the advisor’s most relevant information stays within his head, and the organization stays closed. I recently experienced this first-hand over drinks with friends who work in such a bank.

In large corporations, employees keep secrets from each other everywhere. Executives plan restructurings behind closed doors, salespeople hide leads and the status of negotiations from one another, and one person may hold deeply rooted, old implicit knowledge about legacy software or certain operating procedures. Either way, the result is a lot of implicit knowledge distributed across an organization, inaccessible to outsiders, other departments, newcomers, or the technically unfamiliar.

It has long been thought that this closed organization was a “moral failing” that technology could not fix. That view is now challenged by the claim that generative AI finally fixes it. Before that, Ray Dalio’s radical transparency paradigm at Bridgewater Associates demanded a culture where feedback is immediate, frequent, radically direct, and where coworkers codify knowledge in a manner accessible to all [^1]. Most companies never went that far; the discipline was unaffordable. Now it seems self-evident that companies should create a unified knowledge base through AI to be faster, reduce mistakes, and become more efficient. Companies such as Palantir implement data layers that should induce radical transparency [^2]. Cleansing and codifying existing data becomes much simpler, and agents can scan large amounts of data from the far ends of the organization to make sense of the problem at hand: deals made with one entity in the past inform the likelihood of prospects; managers use the results of previous reorganizations to inform the next wave of restructuring. Tobi Lütke of Shopify has an AI chief of staff, much like many other leaders in a modern environment [^3].

But this is only looking at one part of the problem, because the closed organization is not only a technical problem that can be solved by deploying agents. It is a power problem wearing a technical costume. AI only sharpens it on both sides.

Consider first the individual. Organizational data, especially non-public data, has gained relevance since the value of high-quality human data became clear for model training and reinforcement learning. Buying the data remains of bankrupt companies is an emerging idea gaining traction [^4]; data brokerage is one of the latest hype topics after prominent transactions like the sale of Spirit Airlines’ data [^5] or the settlement for the mass scraping of book data by a US frontier lab [^6]. The more valuable the organizational data layer becomes, the more expensive it becomes for the individual to feed it. The client advisor is not lazy; refusing documentation is rational, because codification is expropriation. AI strengthens the incentive to hoard knowledge as it raises the price of the hoarded good.

Consider next the organization. Not all secrecy is a defect. Among individual contributors, shielding knowledge originates in motives of personal gain. In management it can be necessary: existential scenarios and decisions are discussed there (new business projects, shutting down departments or projects, potential competitive moves), and there, shielding information is also in the interest of the company, as are the necessary Chinese walls between public and non-public information in an investment bank. The organization that opens everything is not transparent; it is defenceless as vital information is leaked too broadly.

Therefore, the agentic organization’s question is less how to structure knowledge in the agentic layer, but rather who may know what. Information that should not be broadly accessible will anyway land in the organizational data layer through the carelessness of individuals, model failures, and the limits of compute given the sheer amount of data. Agents that structure knowledge also review their own conversations and rewrite their own instructions at night (Shopify calls this process dreaming) and may publish sensitive knowledge in unexpected places along the way [^3]. Yet, a “ground truth” across the organization is the whole point of the agentic layer; without a way to establish it, the layer loses much of its use. Model safeguards, as we know them from biological or cybersecurity use cases, do not solve this: when too many requests are rejected, the agentic layer becomes useless. The type 1 / type 2 error trade-off is unavoidable. In the long run, information will be far more accessible within a company unless we establish clear rules of access and make judgement about who can know what.

The sheer number of these judgements will make action necessary. Due to the volume of decisions in the agentic age, agents will have to handle the largest part of them. But machines are bad at taking judgement and responsibility. Like management consultants did for executives, AI can lay out and detail a decision, but it cannot take the responsibility for it [^3]. One emerging field of study will be the cognitive biases of LLMs [^7], just as Kahneman and Tversky explored those of humans [^8]. People have already started performing more heuristic actions with LLMs than with people to obtain better results: launching a multi-agent setup for reasoning among each other sounds like an attempt to avoid “groupthink” and “bring in diverse perspectives” [^9]. If there was one truth to be surfaced through research, we would only need one agent. But there is still ample judgement needed, so we mix and scramble diverse perspectives to enable our own.

Is this a human way to enable judgement? More broadly: what is judgement, really? One definition is that judgement is the decision for a certain pattern within heuristics, in a complex system without clear rules that reacts to implicit cues. If that is even roughly right, then “who may know what” is just the kind of decision judgement exists for. And soon, machines might have to make it millions of times, although they neither hold the pattern nor bear the responsibility.

Organizations entering the agentic age should therefore settle one question before they structure their data: who decides who may know what, and who is accountable when the machine gets it wrong. This decision needs an owner, because “no” is the engineered exception while “yes” is the machine’s natural state: an agent’s whole purpose is to surface relevant knowledge, and it only declines a request where someone deliberately built and tuned rules to decline it. Even Palantir, the vendor of the organizational digital twin, sells mainly this: an ontology that governs how agents, models, and users are allowed to read or change data [^10]. Those rules will in turn be tuned towards “yes”, because an agent that rejects too many requests is an agent nobody uses. If no one owns the answer, the machine will decide for them, and it will usually say yes.
